Four months later · same office

It happened again.

In July 2026 the court asked a different AUSA for a copy of a case cited in a motion to dismiss. She searched PACER, Westlaw and her own files, then told the court the truth. Her best explanation: a Google search, at a time she didn't know search engines were serving AI-generated results that could include "phantom" citations.

You don't have to open a chatbot to be using AI.
For North Carolina prosecutors

You carry the State's credibility.

Judges extend a presumption of good faith to the government's lawyer. The court called his conduct “particularly odious” precisely because of the office he held. The duties are the ones you already have; AI just makes them easier to break.

Before you sign anything

Five habits that would have prevented both.

  • Pull every case yourself.

    If a citation came from AI, a search engine, or a colleague's email to self, open the opinion in Westlaw, Lexis or the court's own site before it goes in a filing.

  • Find every quotation on the page.

    Real case, fake quote was the Renfer pattern. Search the opinion for the exact words. Confirm the pin cite exists.

  • Treat search-engine answers as AI.

    AI overviews sit on top of ordinary Google results. They're a starting point for research, never a source.

  • Run a cite-checker before filing.

    In the second case, counsel acknowledged a Westlaw cite-check would have flagged the fake case. Make it a standing step, not a rescue.

  • If it happens, tell the whole truth the first time.

    Calling it a "clerical error" didn't hold up. The judge found the explanations hard to believe, and the hearing is what ended the career.

The rule

Verify it with your own eyes.

“Always personally verify each quote or proposition with your eyes in an actual case or law or other valid source.”U.S. Attorney's Office, E.D.N.C., guidance to staff, as quoted in the court's reprimand order
